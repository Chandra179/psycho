The tables round numbers to make them easier to read. Scores are worked out between 0 and 1, then shown out of 100. The detailed lines below keep every digit. Calibration is a fixed adjustment so that an average text from the reference texts scores near 50.

Each row shows what share of all words a group makes up, multiplied by that group's weight. That gives its contribution to the score. The score also has a starting value and a calibration adjustment. Up to 10 example words are shown per group, but the percentage uses all matches.

The likely range shows how much a score may change on another piece of similar writing. We checked this by scoring the first and second halves of 375 blog posts. It shows how steady the score is, not whether it is true about you. It is a rough guide, not a statistical confidence interval. Each measure has its own range.

The tool counts single words from its dictionary. It does not understand meaning, \"not\" in context, sarcasm or quotes. Example sentences help you see the context, but they do not change the score.

Big Five scores use weights taken from research on how word use relates to personality. The other measures and the ranges were made for this tool. Research shows links between words and personality, but it does not prove these exact formulas. This is an experiment, not a proven personality test. In a separate test on 2,442 essays, the scores ranked people with an AUC of 0.53 to 0.56 across the five traits. An AUC of 0.5 is the same as guessing.
